    Think recursively

    You don't need to memorise a sequence of moves. Just ask: where does the biggest disk need to go?

    1. To put the biggest disk on C, every smaller disk must first be out of the way, stacked on the auxiliary peg B.
    2. Getting all those smaller disks onto B is the same puzzle with one disk fewer. Solve it the same way.
    3. Move the biggest disk to C, then solve the smaller puzzle once more, from B to C.

    Pattern to notice: the smallest disk moves on every other turn, always cycling the same way (A→C→B→A when the number of disks is odd, A→B→C→A when it's even).

    Stuck? Press Hint. It explains the next move using exactly this reasoning.

    Why it takes 2n − 1 moves

    1Split the problem

    The biggest disk can only move when every other disk is off it and off its destination. That forces three phases:

    1. Step 1: move the top n − 1 disks A → B. The same puzzle, one disk smaller.
    2. Step 2: move the biggest disk A → C. Exactly 1 move.
    3. Step 3: move the n − 1 disks B → C. The same smaller puzzle again.

    Let T(n) be the number of moves for n disks. Adding up the three phases:

    T(n) = T(n−1) + 1 + T(n−1) = 2·T(n−1) + 1

    with T(0) = 0 (no disks, no moves, the n === 0 base case), which gives T(1) = 2·0 + 1 = 1. The "formula, live" bar under the board shows these three parts filling up as the animation runs.

    2Unroll the recurrence

    Keep substituting T(k) = 2·T(k−1) + 1 until you reach T(1). For n = 3:

    Each substitution doubles everything and adds the next power of two. You end up with 1 + 2 + 4 + ⋯ + 2n−1, and a sum of powers of two is always one less than the next power: 2n − 1.

    3Count it another way

    The biggest disk moves once. The next one moves twice (once in each half). The next one four times… In general disk k moves 2n−k times. These counts fill up as you watch:

    4Prove it, and prove it's the best

    Induction. Base: T(1) = 1 = 21 − 1. Step: if T(n−1) = 2n−1 − 1, then T(n) = 2(2n−1 − 1) + 1 = 2n − 2 + 1 = 2n − 1. ✓

    No solution can be shorter. Before the biggest disk moves for the first time, the other n − 1 disks must all be stacked on the third peg: at least T(n−1) moves. The biggest disk moves at least once. After its last move, the n − 1 disks must all be stacked back on top of it: at least T(n−1) more. So every solution needs at least 2·T(n−1) + 1 moves, which is exactly what the recursion uses.
